# Active Recall

Active recall is a learning strategy in which the learner attempts to retrieve information from memory without looking at the source material. It is the shared mechanism behind blurting, flashcards, the Feynman technique, and effective interleaving.

## Why It Works

Because retrieval is harder than recognition, the brain must do more work. This additional cognitive effort produces stronger, more durable memory traces.

> "Because your brain is doing more work, it is more effective." — Justin Sung

## Types of Active Recall

### Free Recall (Blurting / Brain Dump)
- No prompt or question; write everything you can remember from memory
- Low difficulty; easy to perform correctly
- Limited: does not force comparative or connective thinking
- Rating: **B tier** for schema-building purposes

### Cued Recall (Flashcards)
- A question or prompt directs the retrieval
- Generally easier than free recall
- Works best with spaced repetition algorithms (see [[wiki/Spacing-Effect]])
- Still limited to isolated facts
- Rating: **B tier** generally; **lower for professionals**

### Elaborative Recall (Feynman / Teaching)
- Forced to reconstruct a full explanation of a concept in simple terms
- Demands deep processing: narrative logic, prioritization, simplification
- Exposes gaps immediately
- Rating: **A–S tier** depending on feedback quality

### Comparative Recall (Interleaving — Sung Version)
- Tests knowledge from different angles, levels, and in combination with other concepts
- Forces the brain to find the edges of where one concept ends and another begins
- Most effective for schema building
- Rating: **S tier**

## What Active Recall Does Not Guarantee

Active recall alone does not automatically build schemas. Blurting and flashcards are active recall, but they test facts in isolation. Schema building requires **comparative** and **connective** thinking on top of active recall.
