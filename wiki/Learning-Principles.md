# Learning Principles

The meta-principles that distinguish effective learning from the illusion of learning. These principles appear consistently across multiple sources and should be used as a filter to evaluate any learning strategy.

## The Central Distinction: Understanding vs. Familiarity

Most popular study techniques create **familiarity** — a feeling of recognition that the brain mistakes for genuine knowledge. Effective techniques build **understanding** — the ability to apply, connect, and use information in novel contexts.

- Highlighting, rereading, and passive watching all produce familiarity.
- Active recall, schema building, and teaching produce understanding.

Sources: [[wiki/Every-Study-Technique-Explained-in-18-Minutes]], [[wiki/Why-You-Should-Stop-Watching-YouTube]]

## Principle 1: Schema Building is the Goal

**Schema building** is the formation of connected knowledge networks — understanding how ideas relate to each other, contradict each other, and combine. This is what allows complex problem-solving, essay writing, and real-world application of knowledge.

- Isolated facts (flashcards, blurting) do not build schemas.
- Comparative thinking, mind mapping, and interleaving do.

See: [[wiki/Schema-Building]]

## Principle 2: Cognitive Effort = Learning Quality

The more cognitive work a technique demands, the more effective it tends to be. This is also why the worst techniques are the most popular — they are easy.

- Easy + no schema = F tier (highlighting, rereading)
- Harder + active recall = B tier (blurting, flashcards)
- Hard + comparative/connective thinking = S tier (mind mapping, interleaving-Sung-version)

Source: [[wiki/Every-Study-Technique-Explained-in-18-Minutes]]

## Principle 3: Doing > Consuming (85/15 Rule)

Learning is only ~15% intake. The remaining 85% is implementation.

- Watching a video explains a concept. It does not build the skill.
- Starting before you're ready generates specific questions that make subsequent learning far more efficient.
- A blank page is a better teacher than another book.

Sources: [[wiki/Why-You-Should-Stop-Watching-YouTube]], [[wiki/The-Art-of-Becoming-Dangerously-Self-Educated]]

## Principle 4: Start Before Ready / Try-First Loop

**Try → Get stuck → Learn exactly what you need.**

School reverses this: learn first, practice later. Self-educators use struggle as a diagnostic tool. Specific confusion generates specific, useful questions.

Source: [[wiki/The-Art-of-Becoming-Dangerously-Self-Educated]]

## Principle 5: Go Upstream — Primary Sources

Most online content is derivative. Summaries of summaries strip away the nuance and evidence that build real judgment.

- Go to the original study, the original text, the person who actually built the thing.
- Slower, but builds independent judgment rather than borrowed conclusions.

Source: [[wiki/The-Art-of-Becoming-Dangerously-Self-Educated]]

## Principle 6: Boredom and Confusion are Part of Understanding

The instinct to escape difficulty (watch another video, switch tasks) short-circuits deep learning. The brain needs uninterrupted time to reorganize itself. Breakthroughs arrive *after* the boredom.

- Related: the 23-minute focus recovery cost after social media check.

Sources: [[wiki/The-Art-of-Becoming-Dangerously-Self-Educated]], [[wiki/Why-You-Should-Stop-Watching-YouTube]], [[wiki/Deep-Work]]

## Principle 7: Learning is Invisible Until It Isn't

Learning is a cognitive skill whose effects are delayed and hard to attribute. Bad techniques can feel fine for weeks before the consequences appear. This lag makes it difficult to course-correct without explicit frameworks.

Source: [[wiki/Every-Study-Technique-Explained-in-18-Minutes]]
